# Interview Preparation Guide

## STAR Format

Structure answers as: **Situation** (context), **Task** (your responsibility), **Action** (what you did), **Result** (outcome).

Keep answers to 1–2 minutes. Be specific. End with what you learned or would do differently.

## Ready-Made STAR Examples

### 1. Plan Change 78 — City Centre Precinct Provisions (Complex project leadership / expert witness)

**S:** Auckland's city centre needed updated building height and built form controls to guide intensification. As a major metropolitan area undergoing significant growth, the existing provisions were outdated and didn't reflect contemporary planning objectives.

**T:** As lead planner for Plan Change 78, responsible for taking the plan change from initial concept through to operative provisions — the full statutory lifecycle, coordinating 8–10 specialist disciplines over approximately four years.

**A:** Managed every stage: developed the policy concept, led stakeholder and iwi engagement, undertook precinct-level GIS analysis, coordinated technical modelling, prepared the section 32 evaluation report, managed notification, reviewed and summarised submissions, prepared hearing reports, and acted as council witness for three weeks before four Independent Commissioners. Negotiated building heights at expert conferencing, prepared a legal memo assessing NPS implications for the panel (recommendation concurred with by commissioners), and managed the post-hearing process to make provisions operative.

**R:** Provisions introducing higher building heights and reformed built form controls for the Auckland City Centre made operative. No appeals lodged — across contested height provisions in New Zealand's largest city centre. Now recognised as the department subject matter expert for city centre policy.

**Use for:** "Tell me about a complex project you led end-to-end", "Describe a time you managed competing stakeholder interests", "Tell me about a time you had to defend your professional judgment under scrutiny", "How do you manage a long-term project over multiple years?"

### 2. Ports of Auckland Dredging Consent — Jurisdictional Complexity (Navigating a difficult regulatory problem)

**S:** A publicly notified resource consent application for the Rangitoto Channel capital and maintenance dredging was a complex, multi-submitter matter involving contested cultural effects (iwi opposition) and an unresolved jurisdictional boundary between the Resource Management Act and EPA processes.

**T:** As processing planner, responsible for preparing Council's full planning assessment and representing Council at the hearing.

**A:** Carefully mapped the statutory jurisdictional boundary between the RMA and EPA regimes. In hearing evidence, clearly established that the disposal component of the dredging operation fell outside Council's consent jurisdiction — providing a clean legal basis for the hearing to proceed. Coordinated input from specialist disciplines and supported the negotiation process between the applicant and submitters, including iwi.

**R:** The consent was ultimately resolved by Environment Court consent order following settlement between the applicant and submitters. The clear jurisdictional analysis in evidence provided a solid legal foundation for resolution and avoided a drawn-out jurisdictional dispute.

**Use for:** "Tell me about a time you handled a legally or politically complex situation", "Describe a time you had to work through an ambiguous regulatory problem", "Tell me about a time you dealt with competing interests"

### 3. AI Tooling in Policy Practice (Innovation / technology)

**S:** Policy work involved managing large volumes of background documents, drafting plan provisions, reviewing memos, and tracking project progress across multiple concurrent plan changes — all time-intensive tasks typically handled manually.

**T:** Identify whether emerging generative AI tools could increase efficiency without compromising the quality or defensibility of professional work.

**A:** Built AI-powered workflows using Claude Code, NotebookLM, and ChatGPT: document synthesis pipelines for large background reports, structured workflows for provision drafting and self-review, and project management automation for tracking plan change milestones. Tested, iterated, and progressively integrated these into daily practice.

**R:** Significantly reduced time spent on synthesis and first-draft tasks, freeing up capacity for substantive analysis, stakeholder engagement, and expert judgment. Became a resource for colleagues on AI-augmented planning practice. The tools were built proactively — no one asked for them — which reflects the broader approach of identifying and solving problems before being directed to.

**Use for:** "Tell me about a time you introduced an innovation or improved a process", "How have you used technology to solve a problem?", "Tell me about a time you showed initiative without being asked"

### 4. Plan-Stamping Process Improvement (Initiative / continuous improvement — early career)

**S:** As a new assistant planner, observed that routine administrative tasks — particularly plan-stamping — were consistently occupying planner time that could have been spent on substantive consent work.

**T:** Identify and fix the inefficiency, ideally without requiring significant management involvement for each step.

**A:** Mapped the existing workflow, identified where planners were dependent on admin support for tasks they could do themselves, and developed a self-service plan-stamping process. Worked through the practicalities of rollout and implemented it across the consents team.

**R:** Freed up planner time for substantive work. The process was adopted team-wide. Established early in the career a pattern of identifying and solving systemic inefficiencies rather than just working around them.

**Use for:** "Tell me about a time you identified and solved a problem proactively", "Tell me about an improvement you made to a process", "How did you develop in your early career?"

### 5. Building Height Negotiation at Expert Conferencing (Negotiation under pressure)

**S:** As part of PC78 hearings, expert conferencing was convened to resolve contested building height provisions across key Auckland City Centre precincts. There were substantive differences between Council's position and those of submitters, including property developers with significant commercial interests in the outcome.

**T:** Represent Council's planning position at conferencing and negotiate height outcomes that remained aligned with Council's planning objectives.

**A:** Prepared thoroughly for each precinct — understood the technical and planning rationale behind every proposed height, identified which elements were non-negotiable and where flexibility existed, and engaged constructively with opposing planning experts to find areas of agreement. Maintained Council's core position on heights central to the policy objectives.

**R:** Secured negotiated outcomes aligned with Council's planning objectives. The heights agreed at expert conferencing were adopted in the final provisions, which were later made operative with no appeals.

**Use for:** "Tell me about a time you had to negotiate a difficult outcome", "Describe a time you had to hold your position under commercial or stakeholder pressure", "How do you approach negotiation?"

### 6. BJJ Coaching — Curriculum Design and Adaptive Instruction (Leadership / teaching)

**S:** Tu Kaha BJJ needed a structured fundamentals programme for new and early-stage students. Classes had mixed ability levels, making it difficult to run sessions that were useful for both beginners and more advanced students.

**T:** Design and deliver a structured fundamentals curriculum that would work for mixed-ability groups and produce measurable progression.

**A:** Designed a fundamentals programme covering core techniques in a logical sequence, with progressions for different ability levels. Adapted delivery in each session based on who was present — adjusting pacing, language, and demonstrations to meet beginners where they were while keeping experienced students engaged. Coached twice weekly, including stepping up to cover larger classes (20–30 students) when needed.

**R:** The club now has significantly more blue belts and technically skilled students than when coaching began. Strong retention among promoted students. Progressed personally from purple to black belt during the coaching period.

**Use for:** "Tell me about a time you taught or developed others", "Describe your leadership style", "Tell me about a time you designed a programme or curriculum", "What experience do you have with coaching or mentoring?"

## Common Tough Questions

### "Why are you looking internationally / leaving Auckland Council?"
> Frame proactively: "I've had an exceptional 10 years at Auckland Council — including leading Plan Change 78 through to operative — but I'm at a stage where I want to test my skills in a different planning context, work with different policy frameworks, and contribute to planning practice at an international level. Auckland Council has been a fantastic foundation, and I'm looking for the next challenge."

### "You don't have [specific international jurisdiction] experience."
> "You're right that my statutory practice is grounded in the New Zealand RMA framework. But the fundamentals of statutory plan-making — evidence-based policy development, cost-benefit analysis, hearing processes, and defensible decision-making — are structurally very similar across common law jurisdictions. I've actively researched [UK/Australian/etc.] planning frameworks and I'm confident in my ability to learn the local specifics quickly, as I've done before when moving from consent processing into policy planning."

### "Where do you see yourself in 5 years?"
> "Leading complex policy projects at a senior or management level — ideally in a role that sits at the intersection of planning policy, spatial analysis, and technology. I'm particularly interested in how generative AI and GovTech tools are changing the way planning policy is developed and evaluated, and I'd like to be contributing to that shift, not just observing it."

### "What's your biggest weakness?"
> "I have a strong preference for thorough analysis over rapid judgment calls. In a statutory planning environment that's a genuine strength — decisions need to be defensible — but I've learned to recognise when a call needs to be made with the information available and not to wait for perfect certainty. I've consciously worked on that through expert conferencing and hearings, where you have to hold a position and respond on your feet."

### "Why this company specifically?"
> Customize per company. Must reference: specific work, policy areas, team structure, or values. Never give a generic answer.

## Questions You Should Ask Interviewers

### About the Role
- "What does a typical week look like in this role?"
- "What would success look like in the first 6–12 months?"
- "What's the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?"
- "How is this role positioned relative to the policy development lifecycle — are you involved from scoping through to implementation, or at specific stages?"

### About the Team
- "How big is the team, and how is work typically allocated?"
- "How do you coordinate across specialist disciplines on complex projects?"
- "How do you onboard people who are coming from a different planning jurisdiction?"

### About International Equivalencies (for non-NZ roles)
- "How does the [local plan / development plan] process compare to what I'd expect from a statutory plan change in terms of the evidence base and hearing processes?"
- "What's the biggest adjustment you've seen planners make when coming from different jurisdictions?"

### About Culture
- "How would you describe the team culture?"
- "What does professional development look like here?"
- "What do people who thrive in this role tend to have in common?"
- "How does the team stay current with changes to planning legislation and national policy?"

## Phone/Video Interview Tips
- Have STAR examples written out (use this file)
- Keep a glass of water nearby
- Smile when speaking (it changes your tone)
- Ask for clarification if a question is vague
- It's OK to take 5 seconds to think before answering
- End with: "Is there anything else you'd like to know about my background?"
- For international roles: have a short sentence ready on why you're interested in that specific country/city

## After the Application (Best Practice)

### Follow-Up Etiquette
- **Don't call to "stand out"** or to learn more about the role post-submission
- If the employer specified a timeline, respect it and wait
- If no timeline was given and 2+ weeks have passed, a brief check-in is acceptable
- If you have genuinely new, relevant information to share, a short follow-up is fine

### Thank-You Notes
- When you receive any update (interview invitation, rejection, or status update), send a brief thank-you
- Express appreciation for their time and the process
- Keep it short (2–3 sentences)

## Roleplay Guidelines
When asked for interview practice:
1. Ask which role/company to simulate
2. Start with easy warm-up questions ("Tell me about yourself")
3. Progress to role-specific technical questions
4. Include 1–2 behavioral questions using the competencies from the job posting
5. End with a tough question or curveball
6. After each answer, give brief feedback: what worked, what to sharpen
7. Suggest which STAR example would work best for each question
